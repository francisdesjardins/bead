The essentials

Bead is a binary protocol for calls between a client and a server. A request receives its result or its failure, a subscription its notifications, and the server can call its client in turn. A large payload goes in chunks, and a call can be cancelled on the way. It all fits in one small envelope, on any transport that delimits its messages. Here is a whole request, byte by byte.

Bead’s choices

Bead serves where a client and its server hold a conversation. The server pushes changes, a large file goes up while other calls carry on, a call stops halfway, and the server sometimes asks the client something.

Three aims guide each choice. A decoder written by hand reads each field at its place, parsing nothing. A payload is bounded only by the limits of both ends. A refusal says how far the request got.

Choice What Bead does What it gives
The unit Raw bytes behind an 8-byte prefix A codec written by hand, with no serialization framework
The length None in the header: the transport delimits the envelope No ceiling, and a large payload goes as a transfer
The fields One field a byte, or an aligned word Each field read whole at its offset; a flag is one bit to test
The failure Once, in the payload of a FAILURE One place to read it
The codes In blocks of 16, by the stage reached A peer that does not know a code knows its stage
The header 12 bytes for a request, 16 for a chunk or a credit, 8 for the others, before its blocks A size known from the kind, the flags and a credential’s length

Bead takes its ideas from public protocols. The transport delimits the messages, as WebSocket does. An odd or an even callId says who opened the call, as HTTP/2’s streams do. Failure codes go in blocks, as HTTP’s status classes do.

The envelope travels over WebSocket, over HTTP where WebSocket cannot pass, through a Web Worker in the page, through memory between two routers of one process, or across an MQTT 5 or AMQP 1.0 broker. Both ends keep the same API, whatever the transport. gRPC-Web and Connect carry unary calls and server streams to a browser; Bead also carries the server’s calls to its client, and subscriptions at the client’s pace.

The envelope

Every envelope opens with the same 8-byte prefix. The kind and the flags add fields at fixed offsets, then the payload runs to the end of the message.

In network order, the prefix holds the layout (0xA1), the kind, the flags, the header’s length in 4-byte words, and the callId on 4 bytes. A REQUEST adds its service and its procedure, 2 bytes each.

The flags of the low nibble add blocks, in the order of their bits. DESTINATION (0x02) names a relay’s target in 4 bytes. TRANSFER (0x04) gives a transfer’s total in 8 bytes. CREDENTIAL (0x08) comes last, the only one of variable length: a scheme, a length, the bytes, then zeros up to the next word, like a TLS extension. The flags of the high nibble add nothing: SUBSCRIPTION (0x10), PACED (0x20) and MORE (0x40). The bits 0x01 and 0x80 are reserved. A header holds 1,020 bytes at most (§2 to §4).

A peer dispatches every envelope it can read by its kind. A REQUEST passes its gates, then goes to its procedure. A RESULT or a FAILURE settles the call waiting on that callId. A NOTIFICATION goes to that callId’s subscription. A CHUNK takes its place in its transfer, and a CREDIT moves a transfer being sent, or a paced subscription, forward. A CANCEL makes the peer forget the call. A command, the server’s request to its client, is just a REQUEST on an even callId.

The callId pairs everything

Every answer, notification, chunk, credit or cancel carries its call’s callId. An odd callId comes from a call the client opened, an even one from a call the server opened. The two sides thus draw from two separate halves, and the server can call its client too. Such a call is a command.

Each side walks through its half of the callIds, about 231, skipping those in use or reserved. At 10,000 calls a second, a lap takes two and a half days. When every one is taken, the call fails on the spot with CAPACITY_EXCEEDED.

A peer has the other answer 64 calls at once on a connection, maximumAnsweringCalls, as HTTP/2 bounds the streams a peer opens. Nothing announces this number: both ends set it alike. The requester keeps to it, and the receiver refuses one more request with CAPACITY_EXCEEDED before reading anything past its prefix (§9.2).

An answer goes once, and only while its call is open. One that arrives after a cancel or a close goes nowhere, so that it never settles someone else’s call.

A failure travels once

A failure is the payload of a FAILURE, and nowhere else. It holds a 2-byte code, the number of relays it crossed, a byte of flags, then a readable detail in UTF-8.

The flags’ bit 0x01 says the code belongs to the procedure, so a service’s code 19 never passes for PROCEDURE_UNKNOWN. The detail holds 1,024 bytes at most by default, maximumDetailBytes. A failure a side answers for an error of its own, a resolver that throws for instance, carries no detail: the error stays with that side, reported to its application.

{{bytes of="failure"}}

The protocol’s codes go in blocks of 16, by the stage reached: the envelope, the route, the admission, the payload, the procedure. A peer that does not know a code knows its stage at least. Each failure meets one of four fates. A code marked with two meets one or the other, according to the side that raises it.

  • answeredsent to the peer, on the request’s callId
  • reportedraised locally, never sent
  • rejectedthe call fails before it goes
  • gateanswered if the gate allows it, reported otherwise

What does not read

An envelope that does not read has no callId to trust. Answering it at random could settle someone else’s call, so everything goes through one gate (§9.6).

A REQUEST whose prefix reads, of the peer’s parity and on a free callId, receives a FAILURE on that callId. A RESULT or a FAILURE that does not read fails the call it aimed at, and the peer receives a CANCEL. A CHUNK that does not read ends its transfer with CHUNK_INVALID. Everything else is reported locally, never answered. What arrives late on a reserved callId is dropped in silence.

A payload’s rules

A request must often hold more than its encoding says: a count of 10 at most, a name of 1 to 63 characters. Those rules are written in the .proto with protovalidate’s options, the protobuf ecosystem’s standard, and beadc compiles them into native validators, in TypeScript, in C# and in Rust.

The server judges each request once its payload is read, before its resolver. A request its rules refuse receives PAYLOAD_INVALID (52), and its resolver never sees it. A client can also judge its value before sending, with the validates option: the call then fails on the spot, with the same code and the same detail. The rules judge the payload alone. A name already taken, a balance or a right stay with the resolver, which answers a failure of its procedure.

What beadc compiles, and how a pattern is judged

beadc compiles required and ignore; a constant, on any scalar or an enum; a number’s bounds and a float’s finiteness; a list of values, for a number, a string or an enum; the lengths, prefix, suffix and contents of a string or of bytes, and a string’s pattern; an enum’s defined values; a list’s and a map’s rules; and oneof. It refuses any other rule, CEL and a pattern of bytes among them, VALIDATE_RULE_UNSUPPORTED, and ignores none: a rule written and not played would let through what a protovalidate server refuses.

A pattern follows RE2’s syntax and is judged in linear time, with no backtracking (CWE-1333). beadc builds its automaton ahead, from its own Unicode tables, and each validator reads one cell a character. Violations follow an order Bead fixes, field by field. The order, the paths and the detail’s format are in §18.

Cancel

A requester that gives up on a call tells the peer with a CANCEL, 8 bytes on the call’s callId. A CANCEL is never answered (§10).

{{bytes of="cancel"}}

It sends one when its timeout expires, when its signal aborts it, or when it drops a subscription. The other end forgets the call, undoes the subscription and aborts its resolver’s signal. The requester then reserves the callId for the call’s timeout, 5 s if it had none, and drops what arrives late on it. It is HTTP/2’s RST_STREAM, kept to the requester, as a gRPC client cancels. The side that answers ends a call with a FAILURE, which carries a code, and so needs no CANCEL.

Subscriptions

A subscription is a call left open. Its request carries the SUBSCRIPTION flag, and the server then notifies under its callId (§11).

The request states its intent, as a CoAP GET registers only with its Observe option. An ordinary request to the same procedure subscribes to nothing. The client listens on the callId before it sends the request, so a notification that arrives before the RESULT is heard. The subscription ends at an unsubscribe, a cancel, the connection’s close, its session’s end, or on a failure the application chooses. A connection holds one subscription per procedure and per session.

At the client’s pace

With PACED beside SUBSCRIPTION, the server sends only the notifications the client has granted. The client grants as its callbacks finish, promises included: with a window of one, the next notification once they are done with the previous one; with a wider window, as far as that window past what they finished, renewed at its half. It grants with a CREDIT that carries PACED: a count of notifications, as RSocket’s REQUEST_N is, but absolute, as a transfer’s credit is.

Past its credit, a notification waits in a queue. The server can give it a key. A notification of the same key replaces the one waiting, so an entity’s state keeps only its latest value: forty-five changes to an article, while a callback fetches its data, arrive as two notifications. Without a key, every event is kept, in order. A full queue, 64 notifications or 1 MiB by default, ends the subscription with CAPACITY_EXCEEDED. Nothing is lost in silence: the client subscribes again and reads the state anew.

Sessions

A session is what a call runs in: an identifier, data the admission and the procedure read, and an end. Each call runs in the session its credential leads it to (§13).

There are two kinds. A connection’s session opens by a request, a login procedure for instance, and the server keeps it with a 128-bit token drawn at random. A credential’s session is what a verifier draws from a JWT, or from a credential the transport carries. A call without a credential runs in its connection’s session, if there is one.

The server announces the connection’s session in the CREDENTIAL block of a RESULT or a FAILURE, and only when it changes: OPAQUE and its token when it opens, NONE when it ends. The client presents it only on the call that asks for it. When a session ends and the connection stays, each subscription it bounded receives SESSION_REQUIRED.

A request presents a credential in its CREDENTIAL block. Its scheme names a format, as HTTP names an authentication scheme: a peer knows what the bytes are without reading them.

The call’s session

The server judges the credential, then the admission, on the header alone. A scheme it does not take receives CREDENTIAL_UNSUPPORTED (36), and the detail names those it takes, as a 401 names them in WWW-Authenticate. A credential that does not hold receives SESSION_REQUIRED (32), whatever the procedure wants, as an invalid bearer token receives a 401. A verifier that fails gives PROCEDURE_FAILED. Last, the admission decides on the call’s session, which the resolver then receives.

A procedure never receives its call’s bearer credential. The token goes only to the verifier and, through a relay, to the destination. It is a gateway’s model: the one that verifies a token does not hand it to the code it serves.

A JWT, verified one way

A JWT is an access token of RFC 9068’s profile, and every side verifies it the same way, as RFC 8725 recommends. Its typ is at+jwt, which rules out an identity token from the same issuer. Its key is one of those the application gives, each with its issuer and a single algorithm, ES256 or HS256. Its iss must be that key’s issuer, its exp must hold with 60 s of leeway, and its aud must name the peer’s audience. A token never chooses its own key: jwk, jku, x5u and x5c are never read. Its session is named by its issuer and its sid, or else its sub.

A key set, besides the keys

An application can give a key set for some issuers. Bead asks for it when no known key carries the token’s kid, at most once a minute per issuer, so that no peer has it queried at will. The keys received hold for ten minutes. At most 64 calls of one issuer wait for its next ask, then the next call receives CAPACITY_EXCEEDED; a call that waits on an ask under way is never counted. Bead fetches nothing by itself: the function that brings the keys belongs to the application (§13.9).

A credential’s session ending

A JWT’s session ends at its exp plus 60 s. That of a credential the transport carries ends when its verifier says so. The server keeps it only while it bounds a subscription, and a renewed token of the same identifier pushes its end back. At its end, each subscription receives SESSION_REQUIRED. The application can also end it by its identifier, endSession, or end all of an issuer’s whose key leaked, endSessionsOf. It is no revocation: a token still valid opens the session again.

The client’s credential

A client presents a default credential, given in its options, or a credential of a call’s own. A call can also present none. ELSEWHERE leaves the credential to the transport: the Authorization field of each POST over HTTP, or that of the handshake of a WebSocket client outside a browser.

When a call that presented the default receives SESSION_REQUIRED, the client withdraws that default and tells the application, credentialWithdrawn. A failure that ends a subscription already answered withdraws nothing, since the credential may still hold. Until it gives a new one, each call that would present it fails before it goes. No call thus falls in silence into another session. For the same reason, an admission answers ACCESS_DENIED, never SESSION_REQUIRED, to a call that presented a credential.

A bearer credential leaves the machine only under TLS (RFC 6750 §5.3): wss or https. On the machine, the loopback, a Web Worker or a router of the same process are enough. Elsewhere, the call fails before it goes with DESTINATION_UNREACHABLE, and the credential stays. A server behind a TLS proxy declares itself secure by the secure option.

Transfers

A payload whose envelope would exceed one chunk, 1 MiB by default, goes as a transfer. It moves forward chunk by chunk, at the pace of the credit its receiver grants, while the other calls go on (§12).

The REQUEST or the RESULT that opens the transfer carries the TRANSFER block, the total on 8 bytes, and its first bytes, sent with no credit. The receiver answers with a CREDIT: the offset below which the sender may send. This credit is absolute, like QUIC’s MAX_STREAM_DATA, so a credit repeated or late never grants too much. The receiver grants a window of 4 MiB, renewed at its half.

The sender sends CHUNKs, each with its offset on 8 bytes and MORE on all but the last. Each chunk is exactly full, but the last. The receiver places them in any order, drops what it already has, then hands the whole payload to the procedure or to the requester. A transfer of 20 MiB thus goes as an opening, nineteen full chunks and a last one of 324 bytes.

A transfer is refused before its mass goes by. Its opening passes the route, the credential, the admission and the limits before a single byte is granted. A receiver holds four transfers per connection at most, and 256 MiB for all it receives, on all its connections.

The requester stops a transfer with a CANCEL. The side that answers stops it with a FAILURE: TRANSFER_STALLED after 30 s with nothing, CHUNK_INVALID or CREDIT_EXCEEDED for a chunk outside the rules. A NOTIFICATION, a FAILURE and the result of a call that opens a subscription are never split.

The transport delimits its messages, carries both directions and delivers each envelope once, in order. Knowing whether a connection lives is its job too, and Bead has no ping of its own. WebSocket does all of it (§12.2, §15.1).

Relays

A server can relay a request to a destination registered with it, as a gateway does for what sits behind it. The requester chooses the destination by 4 bytes, the DESTINATION block of its request (§14).

The relay terminates each leg. Leg A runs from the requester to the relay, leg B from the relay to the destination, and each has its callIds, its limits and its session. Only the payload’s bytes cross, undecoded, with the credential the relay forwards and the answer. Each refusal falls at the opening, and a CANCEL on leg A cancels leg B.

The relay forwards a bearer credential, and the destination verifies it. Each destination is registered with the audience it verifies, as a resource is named for a token (RFC 8707). By default, the relay’s admission, requireCredential, admits only a credential it can forward. A call with no credential, or with OPAQUE, receives ACCESS_DENIED. Otherwise all these requesters would share leg B’s session, and the relay would lend its own authority (CWE-441). A relay that shares says so in its code: a beforeRoute that checks the destination against the requester’s session, or shareLegBSession.

Leg B Leg A answers
A result, or the target’s failure That result, or that failure counted one relay more
A limit of leg B’s, a payload or a capacity, before its transport has the request That refusal, the relay’s own
Any other error before its transport has the request DESTINATION_UNREACHABLE (17): nothing was tried
An error after, the connection lost for instance DESTINATION_LOST (65): the procedure may have run

These two codes are an HTTP gateway’s 503 and 502, as Bead’s gateway answers them, and what gRPC’s retry rules rest on.

What a relay does not do, and what its requesters share

A relay carries no subscription: it refuses to open one with SUBSCRIPTION_UNSUPPORTED. It forms no chain, since leg B names no destination. Its failures name no address behind it.

All of a relay’s requesters share its leg B: one space of callIds, the same 64 calls at once, the same capacity for transfers. So the relay’s admission, which sees the procedure and the destination, checks the rights. By default, it does not verify a JWT it forwards, so a forged token learns which destinations exist before the destination refuses it. With the verifies option, the relay verifies the token itself, from its first step.

A destination a connection carries dies with it. It stays registered, unreachable, until the application removes it, or registers the newcomer in its place, replaces.

Over HTTP

Over WebSocket, each envelope is a binary message, in both directions. HTTP serves where WebSocket cannot pass. It keeps no connection, so the transport draws one, and each POST carries an envelope (§15.4 to §15.12).

WebSocket does all that Bead asks of a transport: it delimits messages, carries both directions and delivers in order. Each end, where its platform lets it, sends a PING every 30 s and closes a connection left without an answer: a peer gone without a word ends in a close, instead of staying open forever. The global WebSocket, a page’s or Node’s, sends none, and the server’s pings stand for it. A message past maximumMessageBytes closes the connection with code 1009 (RFC 6455).

A POST with no body connects. The server answers 201 with a 16-byte token in the bead-connection-token field, which each later exchange presents, and a DELETE ends the connection. The router, the sessions and the relays see a connection there as over a socket. Each envelope has the type application/prs.bead. The transport reads only its layout, its kind and its callId, at the prefix’s fixed offsets, to hand each answer to its request’s exchange.

The server marks each of its refusals in the bead-refusal field, and only a marked refusal fails the send beyond doubt: nothing was tried. Any other status, a gateway’s 502 for instance, may have come after the request ran, and counts as an exchange lost. A relay thus always tells DESTINATION_UNREACHABLE from DESTINATION_LOST. A client follows no redirect: it would carry the connection’s token where no one judged it.

A call presents ELSEWHERE in the Authorization field of the POST that carries it. Two calls of one connection can thus present two credentials, and a token is renewed without reconnecting. The server takes only the hosts and the origins it knows: security explains it.

What the server sends of its own

A notification, a command or a relayed request has no exchange to go to. They wait, numbered, in the connection’s outbox. The client listens with a GET /bead the server holds until something arrives, 30 s at most. The server then reports a batch, each envelope preceded by its length on 4 bytes. The next listen acknowledges what was heard by its bead-after field, and the rest comes again.

A listen lost resumes where it was, at once, then after 1, 2, 4 s and up to 30, each wait drawn at random. A brief cut costs nothing: the same connection, its session, its subscriptions, and all that arrived meanwhile, in order. It is a long poll (RFC 6202): no heartbeat, and nothing an intermediary buffers.

A client that does not listen receives no subscription, no command and no relayed request. HTTP carries no transfer: a request’s body arrives before its response goes, and no credit could come back. Under TLS, the server serves HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2 on one port.

A request’s timeouts, and WebSocket’s handshake

A request’s head must come whole within 30 s, headersTimeout, or the server answers 408. A body must come at 240 bytes a second at least, past a grace of 5 s, or it receives 408 too-slow. A field that holds a control character other than a tab, or a byte past ASCII, is refused before anything else, 400 over HTTP/1.1 (§15.12).

WebSocket’s handshake is an HTTP request. The WebSocket server judges it by its own hosts and origins, under the same rules, and bounds its connections as an HTTP server does: 10,000 in all, 64 per address. A handshake without Origin, a client’s outside a browser, carries its Authorization beside each envelope of its connection. A page’s carries none, since a browser adds to it what it keeps on its own. A page therefore presents a JWT.

In memory, and several transports

The in-memory transport joins a client’s router and a server’s within one process. The joined transport gives a server its connections from several transports at once (§15.13, §15.14).

In memory, each envelope is copied as it leaves, then delivered whole, once, in order, at a later turn. The transport carries a credential beside a request, as Authorization does over HTTP. It never leaves the process, so both its ends are secure. Each side plays its conformance conversations through a pair of this transport.

The joined transport, joinTransports on the TypeScript side and JoinedServerTransport on the C# and Rust sides, gives one router, one admission, one handling of sessions, and each service offered once, whatever the call’s transport. Each connection stays its transport’s. That is how the HTTP/JSON gateway calls its application’s server. Each transport takes the procedures it is given, every one by default: a request to any other is answered as one to no procedure, PROCEDURE_UNKNOWN or SERVICE_UNKNOWN, before its credential is read (§9.11).

Over a broker

A topic-based publish/subscribe broker is one more transport, and Bead never becomes a network of its own there. Each side holds its core, an in-memory broker for tests, and its bindings: MQTT 5 on every side, and AMQP 1.0 on RabbitMQ 4 for the TypeScript and C# sides (§15.16 to §15.38).

A participant is a side’s link to its broker. Its identifier is the identity the broker authenticates, and its inbox, the topic bead/i/<id>, receives all that comes to it. A server serves a name, bead/s/<name>, alone or in a group of participants that share it.

A broker often joins services and devices already. Bead adds what topics alone lack: calls with their answers and their failures, cancel, each client’s pace and transfers under credit. Both ends keep the same API as over WebSocket.

A connection is placed once, at its opening, and named by its two ends, as a TCP connection is by two sockets. The client publishes an OPEN to the server’s name. The broker delivers it to a single member of the group, which answers an ACCEPT to the client’s inbox. The client confirms with a PING, the third leg, like that of TCP’s handshake. From then on, everything goes from inbox to inbox.

Each envelope travels in a parcel, with a 28-byte header of layout 0xB1. Each end numbers what it sends. A copy the broker delivered twice is dropped before the router, and a loss brings the connection down. An envelope too large for the peer’s inbox goes in parts. Bead keeps its own rules on top: a subscription’s credit, a request’s credential, sent to the server alone, and a heartbeat on a silent connection.

The two bindings, MQTT 5 and AMQP 1.0

Over MQTT 5

Each participant is a clean link, which the broker does not keep past its connection: Clean Start and a session that expires at 0. Each parcel is a PUBLISH at QoS 1. A name a group serves is a shared subscription, $share/<name>/bead/s/<name>. Each packet is counted to the byte under each end’s maximum size. A participant’s will announces its departure to the peers. The TypeScript side goes through MQTT.js, the C# side through MQTTnet, the Rust side through a client of its own, the feature mqtt of its core, over TCP, TLS or a WebSocket. All three are played against Mosquitto.

Over AMQP 1.0

The binding targets RabbitMQ 4. Each link is a fresh connection, and its inbox an exclusive queue that ends with it. The deployment provisions each name’s queue, and the broker delivers each of its messages to a single member. Each message follows a single profile, counted to the byte. AMQP 1.0 has no will: Bead’s heartbeat alone says a peer went quiet. The TypeScript side goes through rhea, the C# side through AMQPNetLite, and both are played against RabbitMQ 4. The Rust side’s binding is ahead. No page reaches an AMQP 1.0 broker.

In both cases, a deployment’s broker authenticates each link, by a password or a TLS client certificate, and its access control lets a participant alone read its inbox. Each binding’s interop runs in CI, both ways.

In HTTP and JSON

Beside the binary wire, a gateway answers a service’s procedures in HTTP and JSON. A page, curl or a client generated from an OpenAPI document call them knowing nothing of Bead. The TypeScript and C# sides each offer one; the Rust side’s is ahead.

The gateway is one more client of its application’s server, through the in-memory transport. Each HTTP exchange draws its own Bead connection, closed when its response ends. What Bead ties to a connection, its session or its subscriptions, thus stays with a single caller: a shared connection would lend the next caller the first one’s session (CWE-441). Its calls go through the server’s router, admission, sessions and rules, like any client’s.

A companion of the specification describes it, spec/transcoding.md, under its own version, {{fact of="transcoding"}}. The gateway listens on its own port, localhost by default, or mounts into the application’s web server.

A call’s credential is its request’s authorization field. By default, the gateway presents it ELSEWHERE, and the server’s verifier makes of it what the application decides. With the bearer-jwt option, it presents a Bearer field’s token as a JWT, which the server verifies. It reads no credential from a cookie or a query string, and offers no command.

The routes

Each procedure a client calls answers at POST /api/<Service>/<procedure>. It also answers GET when the .proto marks it idempotency_level = NO_SIDE_EFFECTS, the Connect protocol’s rule. Besides, each google.api.http rule of its method, the standard grpc-gateway and Envoy read, adds a REST route beside the default ones. An unknown path receives 404, a method refused 405.

The matching, the binding and the OpenAPI document

The path is read raw, with no normalization. When two routes match, a literal goes before *, and * before **. A request binds from its variables, its query string and its body, and a field set twice is refused. A PATCH with no update_mask gets one from its body’s keys, as AIP-134 has it.

beadc writes the route table of the TypeScript and C# sides, and one OpenAPI 3.1.1 document of the procedures an output’s gateway selects, the whole blueprint by default, the same bytes whatever writes it. The gateway serves it at /openapi.json, with an etag. A payload’s rules are written there in JSON Schema 2020-12’s words. The detail is in the companion’s §3, §4 and §11.

A message’s JSON

A proto payload is written in protobuf’s JSON mapping. A json payload passes as it is, a text one as text/plain, a raw one as application/octet-stream. The reference libraries do not agree on that mapping, so Bead has its own reader and writer, on the TypeScript and C# sides; the Rust side’s comes with its gateway. It writes one form: no white space, the members in the order of their field numbers, a map’s entries sorted. In CI, protobuf’s conformance suite, in binary and in JSON, plays that mapping on those two sides; the Rust side plays the suite too, its JSON through its protobuf library, Buffa.

Notifications, as Server-Sent Events

A request that asks for text/event-stream in its accept opens a subscription. The result goes first, event: result. Each notification follows with no event name, so an EventSource hears it on onmessage. The failure that ends the subscription goes as event: failure. The gateway paces the subscription at the rate the reader takes the events, and a late reader holds the server’s queue, never the gateway’s memory.

Its gates and its bounds

The gateway judges a request by the HTTP transport’s gates, in the same order, then adds Fetch Metadata: a request with no origin from another site is refused 403, but a navigation to the document or to the GET of a procedure marked NO_SIDE_EFFECTS. A call waits 5 s for its answer by default, then receives 504, and no field of the request changes that timeout. A request abandoned closes its connection, and the server cancels its calls.

What the wire can do and a gateway does not carry is a row of the support matrix’s “Leveled down”: a connection’s session, a command, a stream’s resumption and its window (the companion’s §2.5, §2.6, §8.3 and §8.8).

The client beadc writes

With gateway, beadc also writes a typed client of the default routes of the procedures it selects, over fetch on the TypeScript side and over HttpClient on the C# side. It reads each problem as a Bead failure, and a stream as an async sequence. The interop calls each side’s gateway with the other side’s client.

Security, plainly

A Bead server keeps itself behind five gates, from the outermost to the innermost. Each gate answers one question, and each refusal says why.

The listening address says who can reach the server. The hosts and the origins say which browser page it listens to. The credential says on whose behalf the call is made. The procedure’s admission says what that caller may do. The payload’s rules say what the request may carry.

Encryption is no gate of Bead’s. It is TLS’s job, under the transport, set up by the deployment. Of its own, Bead only refuses to send a bearer credential in clear off the machine.

The gate What it stops Its refusal
The listening address Another machine, while the server listens on the loopback None: the system refuses the connection
hosts A page that pointed its own name at the server 403 host-refused
origins A page of an origin the server does not name 403 origin-refused
The credential A call that proves nothing when it must SESSION_REQUIRED (32), like a 401
The admission A known caller, without the right to call this procedure ACCESS_DENIED (33), like a 403
The payload’s rules A value the .proto’s rules refuse PAYLOAD_INVALID (52)

A credential, a session

Over HTTP, one says who one is with a session cookie or an Authorization: Bearer token. Bead has both, and a third way.

  • OPAQUE: the token the server drew when a procedure opened the session. It is Bead’s session cookie, tied to its connection.
  • JWT: a signed access token, which each server verifies on the spot, with the keys its application gives it.
  • ELSEWHERE: the credential travels in the transport, and the application’s verifier draws the session from it.

Sessions says how each is verified and how it ends.

Origins and hosts

The threat here is a web page. A page you open can send requests to a server on your machine, and it is your browser that sends them. A WebSocket has no same-origin policy: a page of any site can open one.

origins stops a page of another origin. The browser writes the page’s origin in Origin, and the page cannot change it. The server takes a request with no Origin, of its own origin or of an origin it names, and refuses any other.

hosts stops a page that points its own name at the server, DNS rebinding. Its request then carries its name in Host. The server takes only localhost, the names under .localhost, IP addresses, and the hosts it is told. A server reached by a public name therefore names it.

These two gates keep the server from pages. A program writes the Host and the Origin it wants, and against it stand the listening address and the credential.

What to remember

  • EncryptionIt is TLS: wss, https, or a proxy in front of the server.
  • AuthorizationIt falls to each procedure’s admission, which the application writes. beadc check refuses a service that declares none.
  • RateBead bounds what a peer holds of a server, and holds a slow peer to a minimum pace, but caps no rate. Rate limiting falls to the deployment.
  • A stolen tokenA JWT is good for whoever holds it, until its exp plus 60 s. Bead ties it neither to a connection nor to a client’s key.
  • A payload’s meaningBead bounds its size and judges it by its rules. A balance or a right stays with the procedure.
  • Secrets in the logsNothing a side reports carries a credential’s bytes, only its scheme.
How a call presents its credential

Each call carries its own, so two calls of one connection can speak for two people. A bearer credential never leaves the machine in clear. The client’s credential details the default, its withdrawal and the TLS rule.

A relay

A relay terminates each leg and forwards a JWT as it is to the destination of its audience, which verifies it. By default it refuses a call with no credential, so as not to lend its own session to any requester. Relays details it all.

CORS, over HTTP

The HTTP server answers the Fetch Standard’s CORS protocol to the origins origins names, and to no other. It never allows a cookie: no response carries Access-Control-Allow-Credentials, since Bead’s credential is never a cookie. No browser asks CORS for a WebSocket handshake, and origins alone judges it. A gateway in front of the server that adds CORS fields of its own breaks each page listed.

The address it listens on

A server listens on localhost by default, on both loopbacks, 127.0.0.1 then ::1, and the network does not reach it (CWE-1327). A container, or a server another machine reaches, listens on 0.0.0.0 or ::, and names its hosts. The address keeps the network out, and hosts the pages of the machine itself.

What TLS does, and Bead does not

Bead has no encryption of its own. Its envelopes and its tokens pass as they are in the transport. Over HTTP in clear, whoever reads the traffic also reads the connection’s token and speaks in its name. Off the machine, HTTP and WebSocket are therefore served under TLS, and the certificates are the deployment’s concern.

The refusal event

A page whose WebSocket is refused sees only a close, 1006, with no reason. Each server therefore tells its application each refusal it judges: the status, the reason, the request’s authority and origin, the client’s address and port. Never the target, nor a token. Behind a proxy, it believes X-Forwarded-For only from the proxies trustedProxies names, and from as many others as unnamedProxies counts.

Each server also tells each call its router refuses at the credential or at the admission: the stage, the code, the reason in a word, the scheme, what was called, the connection and the address. What a peer holds of a server

A peer that holds on to a server costs it memory, sockets and time. Each side therefore bounds what a peer holds of it, on the model of HTTP/2 and Kestrel, and each bound can be set.

Bound Default What it holds

A client that no longer reads is dropped, and the server reports each drop, naming its bound (§15.12).

Debugging

Two listeners tell what goes by: one each step of a call, the other each envelope. While no one listens, they cost nothing.

The service manager’s debug event tells each step of a call: its direction, tx going out and rx coming back, its type, its procedure, its callId, what went and came back, decoded, and its duration. Each router’s debug tells each envelope, its header copied without its credential.

With no listener and no registry, a call reads no clock and allocates nothing for debugging: one check per call, one per envelope. A listener that throws never changes a call. Its error goes to the error event, and the call keeps the same outcome and the same bytes on the wire.

A server also says what it holds. gauges() counts, layer by layer, the calls, the transfers, the subscriptions and the sessions. health() draws a verdict from them in the IETF health-check draft’s format: a layer that keeps something for a connection gone is a leak, and fails. Each end-to-end test ends on a server back to zero.

Debugging stays beside the wire, in a companion, spec/debugging.md {{fact of="debugging"}}. A constrained side can leave it out of its build and still conform to the wire.

Evolving without breaking

Every byte the layout 0xA1 does not define already has a fate. A peer that does not know a newer version refuses what it cannot read, instead of reading it wrong.

  • A layout to come0xA2 to 0xAF: refused LAYOUT_UNSUPPORTED, the detail saying the layouts read.
  • A kind to come0x17 to 0x7F: reported KIND_UNSUPPORTED.
  • An advisory kind0x80 to 0xFF: ignored, with no error.
  • A credential scheme to come4 to 65,535: read whole; on a request, refused CREDENTIAL_UNSUPPORTED; on a result or a failure, ENVELOPE_MALFORMED.
  • The limitsNothing announces them. What exceeds the other’s limit is refused, never truncated, save a failure’s detail, cut at its bound on a character’s boundary.
  • Any other bitA flag its kind does not take, or a reserved bit, makes the envelope malformed.

Three implementations

Bead is a protocol: a specification, conformance files every implementation plays, and an interop service. The repository holds three implementations. TypeScript and C# are at behavioural parity. Rust has its core, its WebSocket and HTTP transports and its MQTT 5 binding, and each part it lacks is a row of the support matrix, ahead.

{{implementations}}

On the Rust side, next come beadc’s Rust target, the gateway and the binding over AMQP 1.0, each by a decision of its own. Its WebSocket, HTTP and MQTT 5 transports meet the other two sides in the interop, both ways. The core also compiles for wasm32-unknown-unknown.

Parity holds down to the field. When one platform cannot do what another can, every side keeps to what each can. A 64-bit field, a chunk’s offset for instance, thus stops everywhere at 253 − 1, which a JavaScript number holds exactly. The support matrix, docs/support.md, holds each difference with the test that shows it, and its “Leveled down” register each limit one platform imposes on all.

The sides also measure themselves, on the loopback. TypeScript and C# measure the same layers in the same units: the codec, an echo over HTTP and over WebSocket, paced notifications, a 20 MiB transfer. Rust measures its in-memory calls. On each release’s branch, CI measures each side against the last release, both trees in turn on one machine, and compares them: a regression is read and judged, never a gate.

Generated from a .proto

A service’s code is not written by hand in each language. beadc, the generator, reads a .proto compiled by buf and draws a blueprint from it, a JSON file that describes the API without depending on any language. beadc check judges it by its rules, each refusal at its line and column. Each target then writes a server where only the handlers are written, and the client that answers it. beadc breaking compares the blueprint with the last release, and says what breaks a peer already deployed or the code written against the API. beadc runs in a Docker image, and the example examples/hello plays in CI in both languages, each client against each server. One contract gives several packages, each its own selection of procedures, and each transport of a server takes the procedures it is given: the demo’s Packages tab cuts a blueprint into them, with beadc’s own code.

How Bead is made

Bead is a personal project, kept at its author’s pace. Its method fits in four habits, each one a file a reader can open in the repository.

The specification first

A behaviour is written in spec/spec.md before it is coded, in the RFC 2119 sense, then played by the conformance files every side reads from spec/, keeping no copy. Where no file can play it, each side that carries it has a twin test, named by one sentence in every language’s case: the TypeScript side’s says at connect that it listens, then listens: a batch heard in order, acknowledged by the next listen is the C# side’s Says_at_connect_that_it_listens_then_listens_a_batch_heard_in_order_acknowledged_by_the_next_listen. Every difference between the sides is a row of docs/support.md, with the test that shows it.

A call’s timeout, and the rest of what the specification times, each side takes from a clock it is given; a heartbeat, and what bounds a platform’s own socket, parser, close or connect, run on the real clock. Every rule a gate

A rule that matters is a CI gate, not a convention: the conformance files regenerated and diffed, each side’s public surface pinned to a list, every public name mapped across the languages, every link and every version checked, every action pinned by its commit, every lockfile against the known advisories. Each gate is a job of .github/workflows/ci.yml or a script of tools/, and a decision record lists each rule with the job that holds it. This page has its own: its build must give the committed page byte for byte.

AI agents, in scripted waves

Beyond the TypeScript side, much of the code is written by AI agents working under the repository’s rules — the other implementations, the generator, many of the spec’s readings of standards — in waves the scripts of .claude/workflows/ describe. A change that reaches across the languages first meets a design round in which one agent is bound to disagree, the Tenth Man. Each of its objections is answered, accepted or refused with its reason, in the decision record, under docs/decisions/. Then each language is implemented and integrated, and every gate runs. I decide the real dilemmas and make every commit. The ideas, the protocol’s design and the TypeScript side are mine; where a side departs from TypeScript’s idioms or the spec reads a standard, tests and those reviews checked it, not a specialist, and corrections are welcome.
